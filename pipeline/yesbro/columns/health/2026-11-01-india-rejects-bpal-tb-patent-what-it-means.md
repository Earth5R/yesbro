Tuberculosis treatment used to be a test of endurance. Patients swallowed antibiotics for well over a year and sat through painful injections. The newer option is six months of pills. On 27 October, India's Patent Office decided that nobody gets to own the idea of putting those pills together.

## What happened

The Patent Office refused an application from the US-based Global Alliance for TB Drug Development, known as TB Alliance, for BPaL, a combination of bedaquiline, pretomanid and linezolid for drug-resistant TB, [The Indian Express reported](https://indianexpress.com/article/health-wellness/tb-patients-patents-india-tb-alliances-bpal-patent-application-10941645/). The order was issued under Section 15 of the Patents Act, 1970. The application, numbered IN201817014361, was filed on 16 April 2018. The refusal came more than eight years later, which is roughly sixteen full courses of the treatment in question.

It followed two pre-grant oppositions filed in July 2020. One came from Eldred Tellis of Sankalp Rehabilitation Trust and Ganesh Acharya, with support from the Lawyers' Collective and the Médecins Sans Frontières Access Campaign. The other came from the Delhi Network of Positive People (DNP+), with support from Third World Network, [according to The Hindu](https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/health/indian-patent-office-rejects-patent-application-for-drug-resistant-tb-regimen/article71635671.ece). TB Alliance did not attend the final hearings, [BusinessLine noted](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/news/indian-patent-office-rejects-tb-alliances-patent-for-drug-resistant-tb-treatment/article71634910.ece).

## Why the claim failed

The opponents argued that the combination and its use in a shorter regimen had already been described in research and clinical trials dating back to 2015. They leaned on the safeguards that make Indian patent law famous in pharmaceutical circles: Section 3(d) on new uses of known substances, Section 3(e) on mere admixtures and Section 3(i) on methods of medical treatment. The Patent Office upheld every ground, including insufficient disclosure, The Hindu reported.

The underlying point is simple. Bedaquiline was already known and linezolid was off-patent, The Indian Express noted. Pretomanid was developed with public and philanthropic money. Putting known medicines in one regimen is good medicine. Whether it is an invention is a different question, and India answered it.

Controller General of Patents Dr Unnat Pandit told The Indian Express the decision was based on the merits, and that an aggrieved party could appeal before the High Court.

## Why it matters in India

India has the world's largest TB burden. WHO estimates about 2.71 million people developed TB in the country in 2024, a quarter of global cases, The Hindu reported. The regimen matters too. BPaLM, which adds moxifloxacin, recorded a treatment success rate of 89 per cent against 52 to 75 per cent for standard regimens, and more than 15,000 patients have started it under the national TB programme, [The Indian Express explained](https://indianexpress.com/article/explained/explained-health/tb-drug-patent-rejection-india-treatment-costs-10943445/).

Lawyer Leena Menghaney told the paper that every challenged bedaquiline-related application in India has now been refused, so any TB drug maker can produce the regimen for the Indian programme and for export. She also put a number on what competition already did. "A six-month BPaLM course now costs $284 down from $588 in 2022," she said.

## Who actually saves

Here is the dry part. Patients treated through the public system already get their drug-resistant TB medicines free. So the first saving goes to the government, which buys them. That is still worth having. A cheaper course means the same budget treats more people, and more suppliers make shortages less likely.

What the ruling does not do is set prices, approve generic products or keep a district pharmacy stocked. Patients still pay for travel, tests outside the programme, food and lost income, the paper noted. Ganesh Acharya, a two-time TB survivor, called the refusal a victory and a first step in the same breath. "Medicines must serve people, not monopolies," he said.

## Our view

This is the Indian patent system doing exactly what it was built to do, and it deserves credit for that, even if eight years is a long time to decide whether three known drugs make a new invention. Elsewhere the story is different. BPaL combination patents have been granted in 11 countries and run until 2036, The Hindu reported, and campaigners now want TB Alliance to surrender them.

The legal fight is won. The supply fight is the one that decides whether a patient in a small town gets six months of pills or a long wait. Watch the procurement numbers, not the court orders.
