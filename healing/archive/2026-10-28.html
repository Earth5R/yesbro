Mental Health

Tailored CBT eases depression in people newly diagnosed with MS

msaustralia.org.au

An Australian trial of ACTION-MS, a cognitive behavioural therapy program built for people newly diagnosed with multiple sclerosis, found participants were more likely to see a clinically meaningful drop in depression than those given supportive listening therapy, MS Australia reports. Anxiety and sleep quality also improved.

Read more at msaustralia.org.au →

Compassionate mind training eased distress for people with cancer and their relatives

democrata.es

An eight-week compassionate mind training program reduced depression and anxiety in people with cancer and their relatives, according to multicentre research presented at the ESMO Congress 2026. The study of 503 participants compared it with mindfulness and a waiting list control. Quality of life and sleep also improved.

Read more at democrata.es →

Half of new mothers in a Cyprus study report depression and anxiety symptoms

en.philenews.com

One in two of 474 new mothers in a Cyprus University of Technology study reported symptoms of depression and anxiety that need further assessment. Researchers stress these are symptoms, not necessarily diagnosed disorders, and say perinatal mental health services need strengthening. The same share described birth as painful.

Read more at en.philenews.com →

Pairing rTMS with cognitive load training helped teens with depression more

Bioengineer.org

A double-blind, sham-controlled trial at the Shandong Mental Health Center found that combining repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation with cognitive load training gave better results for adolescents with major depression than either treatment alone. It was a single-centre study of 64 participants aged 11 to 18.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

Young people with depression face higher odds of prescription drug misuse

ajmc.com

Youths with depression had about twice the odds of misusing prescription psychotherapeutic drugs and about four times the odds of prescription drug use disorder, a cross-sectional study in JAMA Network Open found. Boston Children's Hospital researchers analysed 2023 and 2024 national survey data on people aged 12 to 25.

Read more at ajmc.com →
More from Mental Health

Probiotics may lower an inflammation marker in depression, meta-analysis finds

Bioengineer.org

A meta-analysis of eleven randomised trials in BMC Immunology linked probiotic supplementation to a modest but statistically significant drop in C-reactive protein in people with depression. The authors flag an important caveat about the CRP results. A marker moving is a long way from a treatment.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

Which early years programmes help families in poverty, and why

Bioengineer.org

A realist synthesis in BMC Public Health reviewed 67 studies of preventive interventions for children and parents living in material deprivation, a group at markedly higher risk of mental health problems. Instead of asking whether programmes work on average, it asks which contexts and mechanisms make them work.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

Most police officers in England and Wales report stress or low mood

emergencyservicestimes.com

The Police Federation of England and Wales 2026 Pay and Morale Survey found 78% of officers experienced stress, low mood or anxiety in the past year. More than nine in 10 said the job caused or worsened it, and 72% rated their force's wellbeing support as inadequate or needing improvement.

Read more at emergencyservicestimes.com →

Perceived stress partly links job strain to fatigue in operating room nurses

Bioengineer.org

A multi-centre study of 1,122 operating room nurses at nine Chinese hospitals, published in Frontiers in Public Health, found a moderate but persistent fatigue burden. Perceived stress appeared to partly connect working conditions such as job demand and social support to that exhaustion. The study is cross-sectional.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

A psychiatrist on why phone notifications are so hard to ignore

Hindustan Times

Hindustan Times asks a psychiatrist why every ping pulls us back to the phone and gathers six tips for breaking the habit by reframing how we think about alerts. Many people keep notifications on so they never miss a message or seem to be ghosting anyone.

Read more at Hindustan Times →

Why some people say sorry for everything

Hindustan Times

Psychotherapist Dr Chandni Tugnait tells Hindustan Times that constantly apologising for small things is common and may reflect patterns formed earlier in life rather than politeness. She shares four reasons behind the habit and says it is not rare or abnormal. Sorry if this summary ran long.

Read more at Hindustan Times →

P.E.I. extends its mobile mental health program for another year

cbc.ca

Prince Edward Island's mobile mental health program, which lets people in crisis phone a mental health professional and can send a mobile team, will run until March 31, 2028 under a renewed federal and provincial funding agreement, CBC News reports. It had been expected to end in the spring.

Read more at cbc.ca →

Mindfulness and Meditation

A mindfulness program for aged care staff stalled because almost nobody could attend

Bioengineer.org

Of about 1,090 aged care staff invited to an eight-week Mindfulness in Motion program across five Australian homes, 22 registered and 11 began attending, a study in Frontiers in Health Services reports. The researchers point to chronic staffing shortages. The calm was available. The shifts were not.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

Meditation seemed to quiet the palm's faint glow in a one-person study

Bioengineer.org

A study in Frontiers in Photobiology measured ultraweak photon emissions from the right palm of a single person over three sessions, each including a box-breathing meditation phase. The authors report that meditation seemed to quiet that faint light. With one participant, this is very much a first look.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

Ember House wellbeing hub opens in Hove in November

theargus.co.uk

Ember House on Portland Road in Hove opens on Monday, November 16 with personal training, yoga and Pilates, sauna and cold plunges, plus a café and community events. Co-founder Richard Harmer says the aim is to make looking after yourself feel like a natural part of everyday life.

Read more at theargus.co.uk →

Researchers say some breathwork effects resemble psychedelic experiences

minutemirror.com.pk

Minute Mirror reports on a study suggesting hyperventilation breathwork can produce mental effects similar to those reported with moderate to high doses of LSD or psilocybin, including a strong sense of unity. Researchers say it may help science understand how breathing shapes consciousness. The safety question stays open.

Read more at minutemirror.com.pk →

A Harrogate mum pairs postnatal yoga with sensory play for babies

yourharrogate.co.uk

Phillippa Strong, who runs Exhale Yoga and Baby Massage Harrogate, launched Yoga Sensory in September after the idea came during a 4am wake-up with her two-year-old. The 90-minute sessions put babies on a play mat with music and bubbles while parents do yoga. Somebody in the room gets to relax.

Read more at yourharrogate.co.uk →

Sleep and Balance

A one-hour public lecture eased insomnia worries in a Korean pilot study

health.chosun.com

Seven in 10 people who attended a 60-minute public lecture on insomnia said their symptoms had improved four weeks later, a Bundang Seoul National University Hospital team reports in SLEEP Advances. The lecture aimed to ease anxiety about sleeping well. There was no control group, so more research is needed.

Read more at health.chosun.com →

How you use your phone may matter more than how long, study says

thestar.com.my

Semmelweis University researchers report in Acta Psychologica that excessive scrolling was associated with poorer memory and attention, disrupted sleep and balance issues. Weak self-control and fear of missing out, more than personality alone, were key factors behind problematic phone use, lead researcher Johanna Takács said.

Read more at thestar.com.my →

Regular saving is linked to better sleep, UK research says

aol.com

A University of Bristol analysis found people who save regularly get better quality sleep and are more likely to feel relaxed than those who do not, AOL reports. The research, sponsored by Yorkshire Building Society, appeared in a July 2024 report. Small monthly amounts count too.

Read more at aol.com →

A day without smartphones improved sleep and attention in young swimmers

Bioengineer.org

A pretest and posttest study of 43 competitive swimmers aged 10 to 16 found statistically significant improvements in subjective sleep quality and sustained attention after a 24-hour smartphone restriction. Phones went into tamper-evident sealed bags. It was a single-group study, so there was no comparison arm.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

Late nights can undo training gains, a review suggests

The Times of India

Times of India cites a systematic review and meta-analysis by Craven and colleagues showing acute sleep loss reliably hurts workout performance, with strength dipping after one or two bad nights. Late parties and late work both count. The squat rack noticed before you did.

Read more at The Times of India →
More from Sleep and Balance

Heart monitors stroke patients already wear may flag sleep breathing problems

Bioengineer.org

Researchers at Saint-Étienne University Hospital found algorithms run on routine Holter heart recordings with wrist oximetry could flag sleep-disordered breathing in post-stroke patients with clinically useful accuracy. The study in Frontiers in Neuroscience covered 1,929 patients. Most such patients never receive a formal sleep study.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

Body and Movement

Sensorimotor training tops a review of exercise for over-55s

womanandhome.com

A University of Evora review in Frontiers in Psychology found sensorimotor training, which relies on coordination between the nervous system and muscles, was the best way to improve strength, balance and mobility in people over 55, Woman and Home reports. It is often used to treat back pain.

Read more at womanandhome.com →

Wall squats once or twice a week may hold on to blood pressure gains

ScienceDaily

In a study of 100 adults in BMJ Open Sport and Exercise Medicine, wall squats done once or twice a week helped maintain significant reductions in systolic blood pressure over the short term. People who kept training three times a week saw larger and more sustained improvements.

Read more at ScienceDaily →

Digital challenges and streaks may really nudge people to move more

todomountainbike.net

A meta-analysis of 29 randomised trials in the European Journal of Preventive Cardiology found digital commitment devices, the goals and badges behind apps like Strava and Zwift, produced measurable improvements in physical activity and health. The badge, it seems, has a job.

Read more at todomountainbike.net →

Exercise snacks improve fitness but are no weight loss shortcut

Vijesti

A British Journal of Sports Medicine meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials with 414 participants found short bursts of activity, up to five minutes at least twice a day, can improve fitness in inactive people. The caveat is they do not automatically bring weight loss or replace regular activity.

Read more at Vijesti →

Yoga and physical therapy offer good value for low back pain

Bioengineer.org

A systematic review in PLOS Medicine led by Manuela Loureiro Ferreira of the George Institute for Global Health found many non-drug, non-surgical treatments for low back pain are cost-effective, including yoga and therapy. Low back pain is the leading cause of disability worldwide.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

Electrostimulation adds nothing to pelvic floor exercises in young women

Bioengineer.org

A randomised trial of 45 healthy women aged 18 to 28, in BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies, found neither 10 Hz nor 50 Hz endovaginal electrostimulation improved muscle activity beyond pelvic floor exercises alone. The authors describe it as a preliminary, single-centre study. The exercises held their own.

Three popular recovery trends that did not pan out in the lab

outsideonline.com

An Outside columnist shares three studies with disappointing results for popular recovery trends, arguing that writing about negative findings helps guard against publication bias. If readers only hear about the one study in ten that worked, the columnist writes, it looks like everything works.

Why strength training belongs alongside cardio for weight loss

health.chosun.com

Northwell Health sports medicine specialist Russell Camhi told HealthDay that strength training works large muscle groups and, at the right intensity, may burn more calories than steady cardio, Health Chosun reports. Muscle also burns more energy at rest than fat, according to Cleveland Clinic.

Read more at health.chosun.com →

Exercise and women's sexual health get a closer look

ynetnews.com

Ynet reviews a 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in The Journal of Sexual Medicine on whether exercise programs can improve women's sexual function and pleasure. Intervention studies measure what changes after people start training, a subject still largely missing from everyday conversations about fitness.

Read more at ynetnews.com →

Hannibal launches strength and walking programs for older adults

hannibal.net

Hannibal Parks and Recreation and the University of Missouri Extension are starting two exercise programs at the Admiral Coontz Recreation Center. Stay Strong, Stay Healthy Level 2 meets Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays from Nov. 2 to Dec. 18 and aims to reduce risks linked to frailty and falls.

Elizabeth Day trains her arms every single day

womenshealthmag.com

Author and How To Fail host Elizabeth Day, 47, says she does not split workouts by body part and does arms daily, Women's Health UK reports. She thought she hated exercise at school, found spin and yoga in her 30s and fell for strength training in her 40s.

Food and Nutrition

Indian studies suggest diet can blunt inherited heart risk

telanganatoday.com

Research by Hyderabad's CCMB and ICMR-NIN, plus a Chennai-led study in PLOS ONE, indicates that inherited risk of high cholesterol and heart disease in Indians is not fixed, Telangana Today reports. A healthy diet and lifestyle can largely neutralise genetic predisposition, while junk food can trigger the risk.

Read more at telanganatoday.com →

Daily peanut butter linked to stronger legs in older adults

thestandard.com.hk

Deakin University's Cookie Study randomised 120 adults aged 66 to 89 at higher risk of falls. After six months, those eating 43 grams of natural peanut butter daily completed a five-times sit-to-stand test significantly faster than the control group, The Standard reports. The spoon counts as equipment now.

Read more at thestandard.com.hk →

Health claims on packaged food do not always tell the full story

Hindustan Times

Experts tell Hindustan Times that labels like '100% pure' or 'no palm oil' on packaged foods can reassure shoppers without signalling real nutritional value. They argue for nutritional literacy over picking products off the shelf at face value. The back of the pack is where the reading is.

Read more at Hindustan Times →

A gut-friendly eating pattern is linked to lower knee arthritis risk

mindbodygreen.com

Researchers followed 97,569 UK Biobank adults without osteoarthritis for a median of 11.6 years and scored their diets on the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota. Eating more gut-friendly foods was linked to lower knee arthritis risk, mindbodygreen reports. It is an observational link rather than proof.

Read more at mindbodygreen.com →

Low testosterone may make fructose harder on the liver, in mice

ScienceDaily

Osaka Metropolitan University scientists found low testosterone and a high-fructose diet worked together to worsen fatty liver disease in mice, with changes in gut bacteria appearing to drive the effect. The work is in mice, so it points to a possible prevention target rather than a treatment for people.

Read more at ScienceDaily →

Food Politics by Marion Nestle highlights a synthesis arguing that beef in balanced diets supplies nutrient-dense protein without consistently worsening cardiometabolic risk markers in controlled trials. Pakistani and Saudi dates compared nutrient by nutrient

A Food Chemistry: X study analysed 14 Pakistani and six Saudi Arabian date cultivars under one lab protocol. It found high variability between cultivars but no overall significant difference in most macronutrients between the two countries. The date varies more by name than by passport.

Longevity

Healthy diets linked to slower biological ageing, with no single winner

Bioengineer.org

An observational analysis of 7,504 German adults found adherence to each of 10 healthy dietary patterns was associated with slower biological ageing measured by DNA methylation. The links were modest and no one diet came out on top. Rhineland Study findings were confirmed in the EPIC-Potsdam Study.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

A Sardinian village outside the blue zone shows striking survival among older women

news-medical.net

A study in Frontiers in Aging built life tables for Bolotana, a small mountain village in central Sardinia, from validated records for 2021 to 2025. It found a pronounced late-life survival advantage among the village's women, prompting researchers to ask whether it deserves recognition as a new blue zone.

Read more at news-medical.net →

Immature brain cells may help explain why some brains resist Alzheimer's

ScienceDaily

Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience researchers found a rare group of immature brain cells remains present even in people over 80, but behaves very differently in those who develop dementia and those who stay cognitively healthy. It may help explain why some people with Alzheimer's brain changes stay sharp.

Read more at ScienceDaily →

Mayo Clinic studies target the zombie cells linked to ageing

ScienceDaily

Seven Mayo Clinic studies shed light on senescent cells, which stop dividing but keep releasing inflammatory substances. They identify new ways to target them, including a natural compound found in tomatoes. In ageing mice, experimental treatments reduced inflammation and improved physical and brain health.

Read more at ScienceDaily →

Gut bacteria account for some of the variation in ageing speed, study finds

ScienceDaily

University of Hawaii at Manoa researchers report in Scientific Reports that differences in gut bacteria accounted for about 15% of the variation in biological ageing rates. Some species were associated with slower ageing and others with faster ageing. The study shows associations rather than causes.

Read more at ScienceDaily →
More from Longevity

Review links age-related gut microbiome shifts to chronic disease

Bioengineer.org

A Frontiers in Nutrition review says gut microbial diversity declines with age and beneficial microbes fade, changes it links to a weaker intestinal barrier and chronic low-grade inflammation known as inflammaging. It draws on human and animal research on conditions from frailty to type 2 diabetes.

Read more at Bioengineer.org →

Dog Aging Project wins new NIH funding for its next phase

stories.tamu.edu

New National Institute on Aging funding will support the next five-year phase of the Dog Aging Project, an observational study of tens of thousands of companion dogs launched in 2018, Texas A&M reports. Researchers will study how genetics, nutrition and home environment shape healthy ageing in dogs and people.

Read more at stories.tamu.edu →

B.C. residents are living longer but not planning for it, MIT study says

vancouversun.com

An MIT AgeLab study finds British Columbia residents are living longer but not preparing for long-term care and housing needs, the Vancouver Sun reports. Most Canadians want to age at home but are not ready for everyday tasks in their 90s, like changing a lightbulb.

Earlier and more frequent acupuncture linked to cognitive gains after stroke

Bioengineer.org

A retrospective analysis of 226 ischaemic stroke patients who received acupuncture during rehabilitation in Xi'an found about two-thirds gained meaningfully on a cognitive screening test. An earlier start and more weekly sessions were associated with improvement, as were younger age and milder deficits. Being retrospective, it shows associations only.
