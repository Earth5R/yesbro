Green jobs in 2026, what LinkedIn's Green Skills Report says about hiring in India

Green hiring is growing twice as fast as green talent, and India has one of the biggest hiring advantages for people with green skills. The surprise is where those jobs actually are.

By Yes Bro Desk · 28 October 2026 · 3 min read

Every year somebody announces that green jobs are the future. This year LinkedIn brought numbers, and the numbers say the future has already started hiring, it just forgot to put "sustainability" in the job title.

The headline: demand beats supply

LinkedIn's Global Green Skills Report 2026, published ahead of COP31, found the share of green hiring grew 25% year on year, while the share of workers with green skills grew only 13%, LinkedIn said. Demand is outpacing supply in every industry LinkedIn measures. Workers with green skills are being hired at 1.5 times the rate of the wider workforce.

The analysis covers January 2021 to July 2026 and draws on aggregated member profile data, edie reported. That is worth saying because ESG News noted that LinkedIn did not disclose full methodology or sample size. Treat the exact decimals with care. The direction is hard to argue with.

India's number is the one to read twice

As of July 2026, green talent worldwide was hired at a rate 52.7% higher than the overall workforce. In India the advantage was about 96%, against 43% in the US and 34% in the UK, according to the report itself. India's green talent concentration also grew 28.7% in a year, one of the fastest increases anywhere, behind Bangladesh at 31%.

LinkedIn warns that growth rates should not be read as a ranking of readiness, because countries start from different levels. Fair. But a near doubling of hiring odds is not a rounding error. If you work in India and have a green skill, employers are noticing.

Most green jobs do not look green

Here is the part most career advice misses. Nearly three in four green hires, 73.9%, went into roles without traditional green titles, edie reported. These people apply green skills inside finance, procurement, manufacturing, technology and operations. Financial services added green skills fastest, at 18% a year, followed by technology and media at 15% and retail at 14%.

What counts as a green skill? LinkedIn lists technical ones like preserving water systems or installing heat pumps, and broader ones like climate action planning, sustainable procurement, sustainability reporting and impact assessment. Waste prevention skills made up more than one in four green skills held globally, and operational efficiency was among the fastest growing in 21 countries.

So the most employable climate professional in 2026 may be a procurement manager who knows how to cut waste, or an analyst who can read a supplier's emissions data. Less poster, more spreadsheet.

AI is in the room too

The share of data centre workers with at least one green skill rose from 4% in 2021 to 14% in 2026, and green workers with AI skills grew 54% a year, LinkedIn said. The International Energy Agency projects data centre electricity use will rise from 485 TWh in 2025 to about 950 TWh by 2030, edie reported. Someone has to make those buildings efficient.

The gaps nobody has fixed

Women make up about 30% of green talent, and at the current pace LinkedIn estimates parity is more than 30 years away. Young people want in, but struggle. In LinkedIn's survey, 57% of Gen Z respondents wanted a climate or energy transition job, yet 54% found it difficult to land one. Among them, 55% cited a lack of experience. Only 15% of workers said their employer offered sustainability training.

What to do with this

The jobs are real. Today's Yes Bro Sustainability list includes an ESG and EHS Consultant role at ERM in New Delhi and an ESG risk consulting role at EY in Mumbai and Bengaluru.

The fix for "lack of experience" is experience. Not another certificate, but evidence that you have worked on a real problem with real people. Earth5R's FIELDWORK fellowship offers hands-on fieldwork in Indian communities, with real conversations, user research and field research, the kind of record a hiring manager can actually check.

LinkedIn's own conclusion is that employers should hire on skills rather than titles, which could grow the green talent pool 3.5 times. That is the most useful line in the report. The transition does not need more people who care. It needs people who can do the work and prove it.
