Rice methane and biochar credits explained, why big tech is buying carbon from Indian farms

Salesforce is buying rice methane credits, Jain Irrigation is selling biochar and Jharkhand wants its own schemes to earn. Farm carbon is having a moment in India. Here is how it works and where it can go wrong.

By Yes Bro Desk · 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

For years Indian carbon credits meant wind farms and cookstoves. This week the action moved to paddy fields and processing plants. A US software company signed up to buy credits from Indian rice farmers, an irrigation company in Jalgaon signed a biochar deal, and a state government started looking for carbon income in its own welfare schemes. Here is what happened and why it matters.

The rice deal

Mitti Labs announced that Salesforce has signed a five year agreement to buy 500,000 methane reduction credits from it by 2030. The credits come from climate smart rice farming across tens of thousands of smallholder farms in India. It follows an earlier agreement with Google, and Mitti Labs counts Cool Effect, Ebro Foods and Syngenta among its partners.

The science is plain. Most rice grows in continuously flooded paddies, where soil without oxygen lets methane producing microbes thrive. Alternate wetting and drying lets fields dry out during safe windows in the season. Mitti Labs says this can cut methane by up to 50% and water use by up to 40% without hurting yields. It says the partnership could save 700 billion litres of water by 2030, roughly eighteen months of Bengaluru's supply. Agriculture causes around 40% of human induced methane emissions, the company says, and methane traps about 85 times more heat than carbon dioxide while lasting only around a decade in the air.

The biochar deal

In Jalgaon, the Free Press Journal reports that Jain Irrigation Systems will supply up to 19,000 tonnes of biochar carbon removal credits to the marketplace Supercritical across 2026 and 2027. Farm waste and food processing residues are gasified into biochar, which can store carbon for centuries in soil. The syngas runs Jain's factories in place of fossil fuel and the biochar goes back to farmers. Supercritical told the paper that by July 2026, 81% of the year's available biochar quota had already been booked, three months earlier than the year before.

The state wants in

In Ranchi, the Times of India reports that Jharkhand's forest department held a workshop on turning existing schemes into carbon projects. The list includes the Mukhyamantri Jan Van Yojana, the Birsa Harit Gram Yojana, a compressed biogas initiative, JREDA's solar mini and micro grids and NABARD's WADI schemes. Concept notes come next. The idea is that programmes already paid for could also earn credits and extra income for communities.

Why buyers suddenly care about proof

The common thread is evidence. Buyers burned by weak offsets now want to see the work. Mitti Labs says every credit in the Salesforce deal will be backed by field level evidence and satellite verified dry downs, using a GeoAI platform built with NASA support that combines satellite radar with ground data. It cites an A rating from Sylvera in July 2026 and another A from BeZero in October. Jain's project is under verification by Puro.earth, with a BeZero rating in its final stages.

That is the right direction. It is also where the risk sits. A rice credit is only as good as the record that a specific field actually dried out, and that the farmer understood and agreed to the change. Satellites see water. They do not see consent, payment or whether the farmer would have done it anyway. Government schemes raise a separate question of additionality, because a buyer will reasonably ask what the credit paid for that the budget had not already covered.

Who does the ground work

Every one of these deals depends on people who can stand in a field, talk to farmers and record what actually happened. That skill is in short supply and it does not come from a slide deck. Earth5R's FIELDWORK fellowship trains people through hands-on fieldwork, with real conversations, user research and field research in Indian communities. For companies counting their own footprint before they buy anything, Earth5R also offers BRSR reporting support.

Farm carbon could put real money in rural India. It will only last if the ledger matches the paddy.
