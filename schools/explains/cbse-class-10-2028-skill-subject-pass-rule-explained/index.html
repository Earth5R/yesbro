CBSE Class 10 2028 exam pattern explained, five board subjects and the skill paper that can save a result

Today's Class 9 students will take five compulsory board papers in 2028, plus a compulsory vocational subject that can rescue a failed maths, science or social science paper. Here is what CBSE actually said and how to choose.

By Yes Bro Desk · 30 October 2026 · 3 min read

Parents of Class 9 students received a useful gift from CBSE on Thursday, which was clarity two years early. The board laid out the structure of the Class 10 examination for the 2028 batch, and the headline is less dramatic than some forwarded messages suggest. The core exam stays familiar. The vocational subject is where the real decision now sits.

What CBSE said

According to The Hindu, the clarification applies to students in Class 9 in 2026-27, who will sit their Class 10 board exam in 2028. It does not affect the current Class 10 batch.

The five compulsory board exam subjects are two languages, mathematics, science and social science. Students will still study nine compulsory curricular subjects, which add a third language, art education, physical education and well-being, and vocational education.

Vocational education stays compulsory. In the words of Praggya M. Singh, Director (Academics) at CBSE, quoted in the same report, "students may opt for either the subject 'Kaushal Vikas' or any one of the 22 skill subjects notified by the Board."

The board also said the subject "Individuals in Society" and the "Modules of CT and AI" in Class 9, and "Environmental Education" in Class 10, will not be part of the curriculum for these batches. If you had already bought a guidebook for those, it is now a souvenir.

The safety net, in plain terms

This is the part families will search for. As ETEducation reported, if a student scores less than 33% in mathematics, science or social science but passes the chosen skill subject, the skill subject can replace the failed subject when the Class 10 result is calculated.

There is a catch built into the choice. That replacement only works for a skill subject with a board exam. The two routes look like this.

  • One of the 22 skill subjects. These include artificial intelligence, data science, information technology, financial markets, healthcare and design thinking. Students must take a board exam in the subject, the marks appear on the board marksheet, and a pass earns a certificate under the National Skills Qualifications Framework (NSQF).
  • Kaushal Vikas. This is assessed inside the school. A student who scores at least 33% gets a school certificate and does not sit a board exam in it.

The Hindu sums it up as two pathways, a choice between school-based assessment and a board exam that leads to a nationally recognised skill certification.

Our view

The easy reading is that the skill paper is insurance. That is true, and for a student who finds mathematics a weekly ordeal, it is real insurance. But insurance is the least interesting reason to pick it.

A board exam in data science or design thinking at 15 is a small, early signal of what a child likes doing. The NSQF certificate is a national qualification, not a participation sticker. And the subject itself forces the habit schools struggle to teach, which is applying a concept to a real problem rather than reproducing it in an answer sheet.

Kaushal Vikas will suit students who already carry a heavy load and want fewer board papers. That is a reasonable call. The mistake would be choosing it by default because it is the path with one less exam, without anyone in the family looking at the list of 22.

Board sources told The Hindu the aim is to help students "focus on meaningful learning rather than solely on examinations." Fair enough. The circular cannot do that on its own. Students who want to see how skills look outside a classroom can try real fieldwork. Earth5R's FIELDWORK fellowship puts students on the ground with real people, real conversations and field research, which is the kind of learning no marksheet records but every interviewer notices.

What to do now

Ask your school which of the 22 skill subjects it will actually offer, since a list on paper is not a timetable. Look at the subject a child would enjoy studying for two years, not only the one that looks easiest to pass. And keep the 33% rule in mind as a backstop, not a strategy. A safety net works best when nobody plans to fall.
