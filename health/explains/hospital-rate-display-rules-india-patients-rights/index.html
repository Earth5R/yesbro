Hospitals must display their rates, what the Centre's letter means for patients in India

The Union Health Secretary has asked every state to make clinical establishments put their prices where patients can see them. The rule is 14 years old. The question is why it took a letter.

By Yes Bro Desk · 28 October 2026 · 3 min read

In most shops in India the price is on the label. In many hospitals the price is on the bill, which arrives after the decision has been made and the patient is in no position to argue. This month the Centre reminded states that the law already says otherwise.

What happened

On 12 October, Union Health Secretary Punya Salila Srivastava wrote to the Chief Secretaries of all states and Union Territories asking them to ensure clinical establishments prominently display the rates they charge, the Press Information Bureau said. The aim, the ministry said, is to help patients understand the likely cost of treatment, plan their spending and avoid billing disputes.

This is not a new rule. It is an old rule with a new reminder. The Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010, and the Clinical Establishments (Central Government) Rules, 2012, require covered establishments to display the rate for each type of service and facility in a conspicuous place, in the local language and in English.

The catch is in the map

The central Act does not apply everywhere. The Centre has urged the 19 states and Union Territories that have implemented it to enforce the display provisions, The Indian Express reported. Places where it has not been implemented, including Delhi, have been asked to use their own regulatory frameworks, several of which also require hospitals to show their charges. Health is a state subject, so enforcement rests with the states.

The ministry also asked states to encourage hospitals to display package rates for foreign patients travelling on Medical or Ayush visas. So the medical tourist from abroad may soon get a clearer price list than the family from the next district. One hopes both get it.

Why now

The letter arrives amid rising concern about high hospital bills, including expensive bed charges and big markups on medicines and consumables, The Indian Express noted. Activists point to private and foreign capital flowing into hospitals and to vertical integration, where one group owns the hospital, the pharmacy, the diagnostic centre and sometimes the insurer.

States are moving too. Keralam will introduce a Bill in the next Assembly session to regulate high treatment costs in private hospitals, Health Minister K. Muraleedharan told the Assembly on 26 October, The Hindu reported. He said treatment costs and room rents have risen sharply, while insurers at times refuse claims.

What a price list can and cannot do

A displayed rate is a starting point, not a guarantee. Nation Path India made the useful distinction that transparency is not the same as uniform pricing. Different hospitals will charge different amounts, and a final bill can still differ from an estimate because of complications, extra tests, medicines or a longer stay.

The cautionary tale is not far away. In the US, where price opacity is a national sport, overall health spending reached nearly $5.3 trillion in 2024, and KFF Health News has heard from more than 130 people struggling with premiums, copayments and medical bills, KFF Health News reported. Prices that nobody can see tend to go only one way.

What patients can do

Before a planned procedure, ask for the displayed rate list and a written estimate. Ask what a package includes and, more importantly, what it excludes. Ask for room rent, consumables and pharmacy charges separately. Keep every receipt, and if a bill is far above the estimate, ask for an itemised version. If your state has a clinical establishments authority, that is where a complaint goes.

The verdict

The rule was written in 2012. The letter was written in 2026. The gap between the two is the real story of Indian healthcare pricing. A price list on the wall will not fix a bill on its own, but it shifts one simple thing. It lets the patient ask a question before the treatment, rather than after.
