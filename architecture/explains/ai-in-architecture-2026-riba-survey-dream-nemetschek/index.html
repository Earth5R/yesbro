How AI is changing architecture in 2026, from RIBA's numbers to robots that finish walls

Most practices now use AI, startups are raising money to draw whole buildings, and software makers are putting agents inside the model. The profession is more productive and no happier about it.

By Yes Bro Desk · 31 October 2026 · 3 min read

This week architecture got a fresh reminder that AI is no longer a side project. A startup raised money to sell finished blueprints, a software giant put agents inside its design tools, and a robot company promised to finish your drywall. Meanwhile architects are still asking whether any of this makes buildings better. Here is what happened and what the data says.

How many architects actually use AI

The best numbers come from the Royal Institute of British Architects. Its third member survey found 74% of practices using AI in 2026, up from 59% in 2025. RIBA says 75% of respondents reported better productivity and 57% a positive return on investment. Then comes the line worth framing. Only 17% agree that their designs are better because of AI.

So the profession has adopted a tool it mostly uses to go faster, while doubting it makes the work any good. That is a very architectural position.

The startups want to draw the whole building

On 29 October SiliconANGLE reported that Dream Technologies, founded by former Palantir executives Andy Elder and Arjun Mathur, launched with $17 million in seed funding led by Andreessen Horowitz. Dream says its models can generate hundreds of design variations in a few minutes. They were trained on data about 100 million square feet of US real estate.

The interesting part is the business model. Dream does not sell software. It hands construction and architecture firms ready to use blueprints and acts as architect of record, which means it takes legal responsibility for the plans. It says it already supports 10 projects in California and Texas worth a combined $20 million. Elder put the stakes neatly. "A hallucination in an LLM is a typo. A hallucination in a building is a structural fault, with potentially catastrophic consequences."

On site, Evertiq reported that San Francisco startup Origin has $20 million, co-led by Accel and Zacua Ventures, to deploy its OG-1 robot for interior trades such as wall finishing and painting. Pilots include two of the largest drywall finishing contractors in the US, with more planned in Germany, Sweden, France and Saudi Arabia.

The software you already use is changing too

For most practices the change will arrive through familiar tools. SMEStreet reported that the Nemetschek Group launched Archicad Max, Allplan Max and Bluebeam Max. Archicad Max adds an agentic AI Assistant, in beta, that completes multi step tasks inside the model. Early beta users of Allplan Max report up to 36% faster completion of selected tasks. Bluebeam Max became free for eligible students and educators in September 2026. Nemetschek stresses that professionals stay in control of every decision, which is also what everyone says right before the first awkward claim.

The look problem

There is a design cost as well. In Parametric Architecture, Mahshid Motamed asks why AI designed buildings start to look the same. Her answer is that generative tools learn from images that already photograph well, so curves come to mean innovation and greenery comes to mean sustainability. She argues context must shape the organisation of space, and she uses the courtyard in a hot climate as her example. Anyone who has sat through a jury of glossy renders with no sections will recognise the point.

What it means for jobs

The worry in the RIBA data sits with early career staff. 61% of respondents agree AI will make it harder for early career professionals to gain essential skills and experience, because the routine tasks that used to train them are being automated. 59% expect staff reductions across the profession.

Yet the jobs on today's Yes Bro Architecture list lean towards people who can run the digital model well. Surbana Jurong is hiring a BIM Architect in Noida and AtkinsRéalis a BIM Designer for bridges in Gurgaon.

Our view is simple. If software is going to draw the corridor, the scarce skill is knowing why the corridor should exist. That comes from time with the people who will use a building, and the climate it sits in. Earth5R's FIELDWORK fellowship is built for that kind of learning, hands-on fieldwork in Indian neighbourhoods with real conversations, user research and field research. A render can be generated in a minute. A good brief still has to be earned on the ground.
