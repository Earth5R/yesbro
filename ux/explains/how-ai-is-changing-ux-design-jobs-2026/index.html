How AI is changing UX design jobs in 2026, according to the data

Nearly every UX practitioner now has AI at work, and the tools keep shipping. The evidence says the job is shifting, not vanishing. Here is where the work, and the hiring, is moving.

By Yes Bro Desk · 25 October 2026 · 3 min read

Every few months someone announces that AI will replace designers, and every few months the designers are still here, now with more tools to learn. This week offered better evidence than hot takes, so let us look at it.

Almost everyone has AI, almost nobody is thrilled

Nielsen Norman Group surveyed 521 UX practitioners about AI at work, and the results are refreshingly unglamorous. Access was nearly universal at 99%. Use of agentic AI ranged from 85% at startups to 36% at nonprofits. The biggest barriers were output quality, cited by 54%, and data privacy and security, cited by 50%. Only 9% reported no barriers at all.

The most telling line is about where AI gets used. It shows up most in desk work, such as writing code and desk research, and least in work with users. Sentiment was only mildly positive. In other words, AI is a time saver for the parts of the job that happen at a desk, and a shrug for the parts that happen with people.

The tools are moving toward code

The big design platforms spent the week pushing designers closer to production. Figma's code layers feature moved into open beta, letting teams build prototypes backed by real code on the canvas. The Figma agent is now generally available, and Figma published a guide on feeding it your design system, with examples from Uber, Granola and Atlassian. Canva released Canva Craft, its first image generation model, which plans layout and visual hierarchy before it draws.

At Figma's first Config in India, the company added 20 new MCP partners, including Linear, Notion and Mistral, TechCrunch reported. More than 30,000 people registered for the Bengaluru event, Figma's largest international conference yet, and its message to Indian designers was that when code becomes cheap, design becomes the moat, Campaign India reported.

The practical takeaway: a designer who can read the code their tool produces, and who keeps a tidy design system, now gets far more out of these agents than one who cannot.

Floors, ceilings and the junior question

Figma's chief executive Dylan Field argued on the All-In podcast that AI is lowering the floor for creative work rather than raising the ceiling, BigGo Finance reported. The same report says Figma kept growing 48% year on year in the quarter after Anthropic shipped Claude Design, a product many had called a Figma killer.

Lower floors are great for beginners making their first screens. They are less great for beginners trying to get hired. Anthropic's own economists found the company has been hiring a smaller share of junior technical roles as hours worked by AI agents grew, and by August those agent hours outnumbered employee hours six to one. That is one company and technical roles, not design. But it is the kind of leading indicator worth taking seriously if you are starting out.

There is a counterweight. Creative Bloq points out that AI companies keep hiring human studios for their own brands, from How&How rebranding Ideogram to Meta asking Jessica Hische to letter the logo for Muse. When the stakes are high, people still pay for taste.

Where the jobs are right now

The roles in today's Yes Bro UX jobs list tell the same story. Razorpay is hiring a Senior Product Designer (AI Builder) in Bengaluru. Salesforce wants a Lead UX Researcher for AI and Digital Experiences. Bosch is hiring a Senior UX Researcher in Bengaluru. Notice how many of the open roles are senior, lead or research roles. The work that AI does least, talking to real users, is exactly where hiring stays human.

Even the startups building synthetic users concede the point. Vectorial, which raised a $3 million seed round to simulate how users will react before launch, argues that research is so slow that teams skip it and ship slop. The fix it sells is faster research, not no research.

What to do with this

If you are early in your career, build proof that you can do the human part. Run real interviews, test with real people and show what changed. Students can get that kind of experience through projects such as Earth5R's mentored product design internship, which has fellows redesign packaging and test prototypes with users. Then learn enough code to direct the agents. The designers who do both are the ones the job listings are asking for.
